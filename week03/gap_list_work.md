# Gap-list item: what drove Uber’s operating cash flow?

**Company/run:** Uber, `runs/UBER_20261108_184111`  
**Question:** Did the increase in operating cash flow come from recurring operations or working capital?

## Filing evidence

Uber reported $10.099 billion of cash from operating activities in 2025, compared with $7.137 billion in 2024, an increase of about $2.962 billion. The 10-K says 2025 operating cash flow primarily consisted of $10.1 billion of net income including non-controlling interests, adjusted for non-cash items, including $4.8 billion of deferred income taxes and $1.8 billion of stock-based compensation. It also reports a $2.2 billion increase in cash from working capital. That working-capital increase was primarily driven by accrued insurance reserves, because liabilities recorded during the period exceeded claims paid. (Form 10-K, accession `0001543151-26-000015`, saved-text line 1086.)

In 2024, operating cash flow was $7.137 billion and the working-capital increase was $2.4 billion, also primarily driven by accrued insurance reserves. (Same 10-K, saved-text line 1087.) The cash-flow statement reports 2025 and 2024 net income including non-controlling interests of $10.093 billion and $9.845 billion, deferred income tax adjustments of negative $4.779 billion and negative $6.027 billion, and accrued insurance-reserve changes of $2.660 billion and $2.819 billion, respectively. (Same 10-K, saved-text lines 1403–1423.)

## Answer

The increase in operating cash flow was **not explained by a larger working-capital contribution**: working capital added $2.2 billion in 2025 versus $2.4 billion in 2024. The filing says net income was the primary starting point, but cash flow also reflects large non-cash tax and stock-compensation adjustments. The accrued-insurance-reserve contribution depends partly on liabilities recorded versus claims paid, so it can change with timing. The filing does not establish that this contribution will recur at the same level. I would describe the CFO increase as a combination of earnings and adjustments, with a material working-capital contribution, rather than treat the full increase as recurring operating improvement.

## What to check next

In the next 10-K or 10-Q, compare operating cash flow with net income again, and check the working-capital reconciliation, especially accrued insurance reserves and claims paid. If cash flow rises while this working-capital source shrinks, that would support stronger cash conversion from other components; if it falls as the reserve contribution reverses, that would show greater timing sensitivity.
